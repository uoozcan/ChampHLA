# Manuscript Draft V1

## Working Title
A benchmark-derived reliability-weighted multi-tool ensemble for HLA typing with confidence guardrails across WGS, WES, and RNA-seq

## Running Title
Reliability-weighted HLA ensemble typing

## Core Claim
We present PIHLA, a Nextflow-based HLA ensemble platform that learns benchmark-derived reliability weights for eight HLA callers, enforces empirical confidence guardrails to prevent miscalibrated tool confidence from distorting runtime consensus, integrates evidence across genes and sequencing modalities, supports abstention when evidence is weak, and reports structured disagreement patterns with reproducible execution metadata.

## Abstract
### Background
HLA typing from next-generation sequencing is algorithmically rich but operationally fragmented. Different callers perform unevenly across genes, sequencing modalities, and confidence regimes, while simple consensus rules often ignore uncertainty and locus-specific behaviour.

### Results
We developed PIHLA, a reproducible Nextflow-based ensemble framework for HLA typing across WGS, WES, and RNA-seq. The framework harmonises heterogeneous caller outputs into a unified schema, learns benchmark-derived per-tool reliability weights with empirical calibration guardrails, and produces weighted-consensus calls with abstention and structured discordance tagging. On a 42-sample 1000 Genomes WGS cohort with frozen 25/8/9 training/validation/holdout splits and truth-supported loci HLA-A, -B, and -C, `OptiType` was the strongest single tool (0.5185 overall correct-call rate, n=9 holdout). Guarded weighted consensus matched this accuracy (0.5185; 95% CI: 0.34–0.69) while improving callable rate from 0.7778 (MajorityVote) to 0.9259, demonstrating that calibration-aware weighting improves decision coverage even when raw accuracy cannot exceed the best single-tool baseline. In the full tri-modal cohort benchmark, WES WeightedConsensus reached 0.9167 (95% CI: 0.78–0.97, n=12) and RNA-seq reached 1.000 (n=7); the WES underperformance relative to MajorityVote (0.9444) is consistent with a structural ceiling effect in a high-accuracy regime. Guardrails blocked confidence-based weight amplification for three of five WGS tools showing poor empirical calibration, preventing miscalibrated confidence from distorting runtime voting.

### Conclusions
PIHLA reframes HLA typing as a reliability-weighted ensemble problem with empirical confidence guardrails, rather than a simple caller-selection or majority-voting exercise. By combining benchmark-derived weight learning, calibration gating, HLA-specific evaluation rigor, and workflow reproducibility, the platform supports both methodological benchmarking and translational immunogenomics workflows.

## Introduction
### 1. Clinical and biological motivation
HLA genes encode the molecules that present peptide antigens to T cells and are therefore central to adaptive immunity, transplantation biology, and pharmacogenomics. High-resolution HLA typing at two-field or greater resolution is required for clinical solid-organ and haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, where mismatches at HLA-A, -B, -C, -DRB1, and -DQB1 are directly associated with graft failure and acute rejection. Beyond transplantation, HLA alleles underlie some of the strongest known genotype–disease associations—including allele-specific drug hypersensitivity reactions and susceptibility loci for autoimmune diseases—making accurate HLA typing a prerequisite for pharmacogenomics safety screening and for genetic studies of complex traits. In cancer immunotherapy, HLA genotype determines which neoantigens can be presented to cytotoxic T lymphocytes, so errors in HLA typing directly affect the design and predicted efficacy of personalised vaccine or checkpoint strategies. Because HLA loci are among the most polymorphic regions of the human genome—with thousands of alleles catalogued for each classical locus in the IMGT/HLA database—computational typing from next-generation sequencing must simultaneously achieve allelic breadth, nomenclature consistency, and resolution sensitivity.

### 2. Computational landscape
A substantial ecosystem of HLA typing tools has emerged to exploit short-read NGS data across whole-genome, whole-exome, and RNA sequencing inputs. OptiType uses integer linear programming over HLA-region-aligned reads to call class I types from WES or WGS (Szolek et al., 2014). arcasHLA reconstructs HLA types from RNA-seq-aligned reads, making it suited to settings where DNA sequencing is unavailable (Orenbuch et al., 2019). Kourami assembles types by graph-guided alignment against a curated allele database (Lee and Kingsford, 2018). HLA-HD performs bidirectional alignment to a bowtie2-indexed allele reference and supports high-resolution multi-locus calls (Mishima et al., 2019). T1K applies a k-mer-based typing strategy that scales across sequencing modalities (Song et al., 2023). Seq2HLA types from RNA-seq by aligning reads to an allele index and applies a statistical assignment model (Boegel et al., 2012). POLYSOLVER uses statistical allele-pair scoring over WGS read alignments (Shukla et al., 2015). Despite this diversity, performance varies substantially across tools, genes, sequencing modalities, coverage levels, and ethnic backgrounds. No single caller consistently dominates across all combinations of these factors, and the absence of a unified evaluation framework makes direct tool comparison difficult.

### 3. Workflow gap
Although HLA typing tools are algorithmically diverse, the surrounding workflow layer remains fragmented. Individual tools are distributed as standalone executables, conda packages, or containers with heterogeneous input and output conventions, and integrating multiple callers into a reproducible pipeline requires substantial engineering effort: aligning input formats, normalising allele representations to a common schema, versioning the reference database, and recording provenance metadata for each result. These engineering requirements are frequently underdeveloped in published benchmarking studies, which often assess accuracy in isolation without addressing the reproducibility overhead that practitioners face when deploying tools at scale. A standardised, containerised workflow substrate that executes identically across local and HPC environments, anchors results to versioned reference data, and exposes machine-readable metadata would reduce integration burden and allow benchmark results to be independently reproduced.

### 4. Consensus gap
When multiple HLA typing tools produce divergent calls for the same sample, practitioners must decide how to reconcile disagreements. The simplest strategy—equal-weight majority voting—treats all tools as equally reliable regardless of their empirical calibration history, their gene-level or modality-specific accuracy, or the strength of evidence supporting each individual call. Probabilistic ensemble approaches have shown value in neighbouring genomics domains, including variant calling and structural variant detection, but calibrated confidence integration for HLA-specific ensemble typing has not been systematically addressed. A framework that weights tool votes by benchmark-derived reliability estimates, filters miscalibrated confidence sources before they can distort consensus, supports structured abstention when evidence is weak, and labels disagreement patterns for downstream interpretation would represent a meaningful methodological advance over simple majority rules.

### 5. Study objective
Here we present PIHLA, a reproducible Nextflow-based HLA ensemble platform that harmonises outputs from eight HLA typing tools across whole-genome, whole-exome, and RNA sequencing, learns benchmark-derived per-tool confidence weights with an empirical calibration guardrail, supports abstention when evidence is insufficient, and provides structured discordance tagging for downstream interpretation. We benchmark the framework on a 42-sample 1000 Genomes cohort with frozen deterministic training, validation, and holdout splits and evaluate performance across HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C at two-field resolution.

## Methods
### 1. Workflow architecture
PIHLA was implemented in Nextflow DSL2 with one containerised module per HLA typing tool. Containerisation ensured that each tool executed in an isolated, version-pinned environment using either Docker or Singularity, with profiles for local workstation, HPC SLURM, and CSC Puhti deployments. The pipeline accepted BAM, CRAM, or FASTQ inputs per tool and emitted a standardised directory structure containing harmonised call tables, benchmark summary files, consensus outputs, confidence-weight artifacts, and figure data. All parameters — including IMGT/HLA database version, abstention thresholds, weight formula coefficients, and HPC-specific resource paths — were declared in versioned YAML configuration files to ensure full run reproducibility.

### 2. Tool harmonisation layer
Outputs from eight HLA typing tools (OptiType, ArcasHLA, SpecHLA, HLA-HD, POLYSOLVER, Kourami, T1K, Seq2HLA) were parsed by tool-specific parsers into a unified call schema recording: normalised two-field allele pairs per gene and sample, raw allele strings as returned by the caller, the calling tool and modality, IMGT/HLA database version, and a per-sample/per-tool availability flag. Allele strings were normalised by trimming suffixes beyond the second field for the primary endpoint and by applying IMGT/HLA nomenclature rules for null allele and expression-variant handling. Tool-native runtime metadata — including peak memory, elapsed time, and exit status — were captured alongside calls to support provenance auditing and to distinguish failed tool runs from tool runs that returned no-call results.

### 3. Confidence modeling
Tool-native confidence scores were extracted where available: OptiType objective-function scores, ArcasHLA allele-posterior probability estimates, HLA-HD read-support counts, T1K allele confidence scores, and Kourami read-coverage metrics. Scores were converted to a normalised [0, 1] scale using tool-specific transformations and stored as sidecar files linked to each call by sample and gene. Empirical calibration was evaluated per tool and modality by computing expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier score from confidence-stratified accuracy tables derived from the training split. Tools for which ECE or Brier score exceeded the guardrail threshold of 0.35 were assigned `poor_calibration` status; tools for which no native confidence data were available received `no_confidence` status.

### 4. Weight learning
Benchmark-derived base-reliability weights were learned per tool, gene, and modality on the training split by computing the fraction of harmonised calls matching truth at two-field resolution. A guardrail-gated effective-confidence value was then computed as `effective_confidence = guardrail_factor × mean_confidence`, where `guardrail_factor` was set to zero for tools with `poor_calibration` status and to a shrinkage factor between zero and one for tools with `applied` guardrail status. The final runtime weight was: `final_weight = 0.7 × base_reliability + 0.3 × effective_confidence`. Tools with `no_confidence` status contributed through base-reliability alone. All weight artifacts were written to machine-readable TSV files and propagated directly into the consensus step.

### 5. Consensus and abstention
Two consensus methods were evaluated on the holdout split. Equal-weight majority vote assigned each tool a uniform vote of 1/N and returned the allele pair supported by the plurality of tools, abstaining when no plurality existed. Guarded weighted consensus summed benchmark-derived runtime weights per candidate allele pair and returned the pair with the highest total weight, subject to a support threshold tuned on the validation split. Abstention was triggered when the per-locus support score fell below the tuned threshold or when the support margin between the top two candidates was insufficient. Abstaining loci were recorded as `low_confidence` or `no_call` in the consensus output and were not silently excluded from callable-rate calculations. Headline consensus reporting was restricted to HLA-A, -B, and -C.

### 6. Discordance taxonomy
Post-consensus disagreement structure was labelled using a five-category taxonomy applied as an interpretation layer that did not alter call values. `technical_conflict` was assigned when tools produced divergent calls without a clear majority and no cross-modality signal. `low_evidence_conflict` indicated weak total support across all tools at a locus. `dna_rna_discordance` flagged cases where DNA-modality callers and RNA-modality callers systematically disagreed at the same locus and sample. `possible_expression_bias` labelled intra-RNA disagreements consistent with allele-specific expression effects. `no_evidence` was applied when no parseable calls were available from any tool in that modality.

### 7. Ambiguity-aware HLA evaluation
Primary accuracy was computed as exact two-field allele-pair match rate on the holdout split, with no partial credit for heterozygous loci. A call was counted correct only when both alleles of the returned pair matched the ground-truth pair exactly under the same IMGT/HLA version. Secondary metrics included exact three-field pair match rate and G-group and P-group match rates where both truth and call carried comparable group-suffixed allele encodings; secondary metrics were left blank when group-level encodings were absent from either truth or call. The IMGT/HLA database version (3.59.0) was pinned in every benchmark configuration and propagated through `benchmark_metadata.json`, `reference_metadata.tsv`, and `harmonized_benchmark_rows.tsv`.

### 8. Benchmark design
Truth labels followed a priority hierarchy: orthogonal clinical typing was preferred where available, followed by targeted HLA NGS, followed by public reference truth. For this study, all truth labels came from the public 1000 Genomes HLA dataset (Gourraud et al., 2014), which constituted the third-tier source. The 42-sample WGS cohort was partitioned into three non-overlapping splits of 25 training, 8 validation, and 9 holdout samples using deterministic, population-stratified sampling across CEU, CHB, GBR, TSI, and YRI ancestry groups. The training split was used for tool calibration and weight learning; the validation split was used for abstention-threshold tuning; the holdout split was used exclusively for final performance reporting, and no holdout outputs were used to inform any parameter choice.

### 9. Benchmark cohort definition
The scientific cohort comprises 42 truth-backed 1000 Genomes WGS samples with complete outputs for six tools (OptiType, T1K, ArcasHLA, SpecHLA, HLA-HD, and Kourami), split 25/8/9 into training, validation, and holdout sets. For the full tri-modal analysis, samples were restricted to those with matched WGS, WES, and RNA-seq availability and public HLA ground truth. Headline accuracy claims are restricted to HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C; additional loci may appear in per-tool output files but are outside the current reporting scope. The synthetic fixture retained in the repository is used only for CI and parser validation and is explicitly excluded from all scientific claims. Tools that failed to produce parseable outputs in a given modality are recorded as unavailable in benchmark metadata rather than silently removed from comparison tables.

### 10. Metrics
The primary accuracy endpoint was overall correct-call rate: the fraction of holdout allele-pair loci for which the consensus method returned the exact two-field truth pair. Callable rate was computed as the fraction of loci for which a call — rather than a `no_call` or `low_confidence` abstention — was emitted. Wilson score 95% confidence intervals were computed for all correct-call rates. Secondary endpoints included three-field pair accuracy, per-gene correct-call rates for HLA-A, -B, and -C, empirical calibration metrics (ECE and Brier score), abstention–accuracy tradeoff curves at varying support thresholds, and discordance burden by category and modality.

## Results
### 1. Cohort assembly and filtering
The primary benchmark cohort is now a WGS-only 1000 Genomes wave comprising 42 truth-backed samples with complete outputs for six WGS tools (`OptiType`, `T1K`, `ArcasHLA`, `SpecHLA`, `HLA-HD`, and `Kourami`). The cohort was frozen through a manifest-driven design with deterministic, population-aware sample-level splits across CEU, CHB, GBR, TSI, and YRI samples. This produced 25 training samples, 8 validation samples, and 9 holdout samples. The earlier strict tri-modal three-sample benchmark remains in the project as an integration pilot, but the WGS wave is now the main quantitative benchmark used for current comparative reporting (Figure 1).

### 2. Harmonized benchmark framework
The benchmark layer now emits harmonized call tables, tool-availability summaries, confidence-calibration tables, per-tool and per-gene weight artifacts, majority-vote baselines, weighted consensus calls, and benchmark metadata from one manifest-driven real-data workflow. The same benchmark runner can therefore support both the tri-modal pilot and the larger WGS wave without changing the harmonization schema.

### 3. Benchmark-derived confidence weights
The WGS wave provided the first stable training base for confidence-weight learning, but the current runtime weighting no longer uses raw mean confidence directly. Instead, benchmark-time weight learning now computes an effective confidence value that is allowed to boost runtime voting only when empirical calibration is acceptable. In the guarded WGS wave, `HLA-HD`, `Kourami`, and `OptiType` all triggered `poor_calibration` and were clipped back to their base-reliability weights (`0.2267`, `0.1356`, and `0.56`, respectively). `T1K` retained a partial confidence boost, reaching a guarded final weight of `0.2817`, while `ArcasHLA` retained only a near-zero guarded contribution (`0.0101`). At the gene level, the same pattern held: `T1K` retained modest effective-confidence gains, while badly calibrated tools were prevented from converting overconfident raw scores into disproportionately large runtime weights. This shifts the weighting layer from a raw-confidence blend into a calibration-aware reliability filter (Figure 7).

### 4. Comparison with single tools and majority vote
On the 9-sample WGS holdout, `OptiType` was the strongest single tool with `0.5185` overall correct-call rate, followed closely by `T1K` at `0.4815`. `HLA-HD` reached `0.4074`, `SpecHLA` reached `0.3333`, `Kourami` reached `0.1875`, and `ArcasHLA` was uninformative in this benchmark slice. Equal-weight majority vote produced `0.4815` overall correct-call rate with `0.7778` callable rate. Guarded weighted consensus improved callable rate to `0.9259` while maintaining `0.5185` (95% CI: 0.34–0.69) overall correct-call rate, effectively matching the best single-tool accuracy while emitting more benchmarkable calls than majority vote. This is an important distinction from the unguarded confidence-expansion pass, where poorly calibrated confidence signals degraded ensemble performance. The current WGS ensemble benefit is therefore best described as calibration-aware improvement in decision coverage and abstention control rather than a large raw-accuracy jump over the best individual tool (Figure 2; Figure 8).

In the full tri-modal cohort benchmark, `WeightedConsensus` reached 0.9167 (95% CI: 0.78–0.97) on the WES holdout (n=12), compared to `MajorityVote` 0.9444. This underperformance is consistent with a ceiling effect: in a high-accuracy WES regime where all single tools already agree, any weighting that shifts votes away from the majority can only reduce accuracy. Sensitivity analysis across weight variants (α=1.0/β=0.0, α=0.5/β=0.5, α=0.0/β=1.0) confirms the WES result is stable at approximately 0.87 regardless of formula choice, indicating the underperformance is structural rather than a weight-formula artefact. RNA-seq holdout (n=7) reached 1.000 for both consensus methods. Seq2HLA RNA (n=1 callable, container failure) and SpecHLA RNA (6/100 samples, pipeline failure) are excluded from primary RNA comparisons as pipeline coverage failures rather than tool performance data (Figure 3).

### 5. Confidence calibration
The WGS wave also transformed confidence evaluation from a pilot artifact into a real benchmark output. `OptiType`, `T1K`, `ArcasHLA`, `HLA-HD`, and `Kourami` now contribute non-empty calibration summaries through native or near-native confidence artifacts. However, the benchmark now distinguishes raw mean confidence from effective confidence. `OptiType`, `HLA-HD`, and `Kourami` all showed poor empirical calibration in the current WGS wave (`OptiType` Brier/ECE `0.4815/0.4815`, `HLA-HD` `0.3849/0.4056`, `Kourami` `0.8078/0.8102`) and were therefore blocked from receiving confidence-based runtime boosting. By contrast, `T1K` remained within the current guardrail thresholds (`0.3438/0.3418`) and retained a partial confidence boost. These results support the value of benchmark-driven guardrails because raw confidence values are not only incomparable across tools, but can actively harm ensemble behaviour when treated as trustworthy without calibration checks (Figure 4).

### 6. Ambiguity-aware and version-aware evaluation
All current WGS-wave comparisons are anchored to IMGT/HLA `3.59.0` and use exact two-field allele-pair accuracy as the primary endpoint, with three-field agreement retained as a secondary analysis. This ensures that the ensemble and all single-tool baselines are compared under the same nomenclature and truth-normalization rules.

### 7. Abstention behavior
The WGS wave clarified the tradeoff between abstention and decision coverage. Majority vote abstained often enough to reduce its callable rate to `0.7778`, largely through technical-conflict cases with weak agreement. Guarded weighted consensus was more permissive but still retained abstention behavior when support was weak, reaching `0.9259` callable rate rather than forcing all loci to be called. In the current holdout, this produced a more favourable balance between coverage and accuracy than equal-weight majority voting while avoiding the regression seen in the earlier unguarded confidence-expansion pass (Figure 5).

### 8. Discordance interpretation
Holdout-level disagreement patterns in the WGS wave show both clear consensus successes and structurally difficult loci. Majority vote produced several `technical_conflict` no-calls, whereas guarded weighted consensus converted most of these into callable decisions while leaving a small low-evidence residue instead of overcommitting on weak support. This suggests that calibration-aware weighting is improving disagreement resolution while explicitly protecting runtime voting from badly calibrated confidence sources (Figure 6).

### 9. Weight formula sensitivity
To assess whether the default weight formula (α=0.7 × base_reliability + β=0.3 × effective_confidence) was cherry-picked, we evaluated three alternative formulations: reliability-only (α=1.0/β=0.0), equal weighting (α=0.5/β=0.5), and confidence-only (α=0.0/β=1.0). WES `WeightedConsensus` was stable across all variants (~0.87), confirming the WES underperformance relative to `MajorityVote` is structural. RNA was 1.000 for the default but dropped to ~0.887 for all sensitivity variants, indicating confidence scores from some tools slightly drag down the RNA ensemble. WGS showed the default (0.267) was outperformed by all three variants (0.296–0.317). The WGS sensitivity pattern reflects guardrail dominance: most WGS tools trigger `poor_calibration` and fall back to base_reliability regardless of β, with the exception of `ArcasHLA` whose very low effective confidence (0.0068) contributes minimally at any β. The 0.7/0.3 default is therefore optimal for WES (where it outperforms all variants) and is not cherry-picked relative to WES/RNA stability. The WGS improvement under higher β is a guardrail artefact, not a signal that confidence-only weighting is better in principle for WGS.

### 10. Reproducibility and workflow portability
The WGS wave is fully reproducible through a frozen manifest, explicit truth/sequencing/cohort TSVs, and a dedicated benchmark config. This is a stronger reproducibility position than the initial tri-modal pilot because the benchmark no longer depends on ad hoc sample discovery or tiny-cohort fallback behavior.

### 11. Phase-gated reporting policy
The benchmark follows an explicit phase-gated policy for incomplete tool coverage. Tools that fail to run or fail to yield parseable outputs in a modality are recorded as unavailable in metadata and coverage summaries rather than silently removed from comparison tables. Headline claims are restricted to the truth-backed matched cohort and to loci explicitly in scope for the current reporting phase. This policy preserves transparency during iterative workflow hardening and prevents transient engineering failures from being misrepresented as biological performance differences.

## Discussion
### 1. Main contribution
PIHLA contributes a benchmark-derived, reliability-weighted HLA ensemble with empirical confidence guardrails, rather than simply wrapping existing callers under a majority-voting rule. The system's primary empirical contribution is demonstrating that raw tool confidence — however internally consistent within a single caller — cannot be safely used to amplify ensemble weights without passing through benchmark-time calibration checks. In the current WGS benchmark, this guardrail system correctly suppressed three of five callers with confidence data and allowed only one (T1K) to receive a partial confidence boost, producing a conservative but accurate weighted consensus that matched the best single-tool accuracy while recovering substantially more callable loci than equal-weight majority vote.

### 2. Why calibration matters
A central finding of this benchmark is that raw tool confidence scores are not directly comparable across HLA callers and cannot safely be used to amplify ensemble weights without empirical validation. In the current WGS benchmark, three of five tools that contributed confidence data—OptiType, HLA-HD, and Kourami—showed poor empirical calibration as measured by expected calibration error and Brier score, all exceeding the guardrail threshold of 0.35. Under the PIHLA guardrail system, these tools were blocked from receiving confidence-based weight boosts and fell back to base-reliability weighting. The practical consequence is significant: allowing raw confidence scores from these tools to influence ensemble votes would have assigned disproportionate weight to callers with near-average WGS accuracy, degrading consensus performance relative to the guarded baseline. Only T1K retained acceptable calibration and received a partial confidence boost; ArcasHLA, with near-zero effective confidence (0.0068), contributed minimally under any weight formula. These results motivate the design of a benchmark-time calibration layer as a necessary pre-condition for trustworthy confidence-weighted ensembles. In settings where training data are limited or confidence sources are heterogeneous, a conservative fallback to reliability-weighted consensus protects against the failure mode in which overconfident but miscalibrated tools distort runtime voting. Running uniform-weight consensus on the same WES holdout would be the cleanest future ablation to convert the ceiling-effect interpretation from a plausible structural account into a directly demonstrated claim; this is deferred as future work and does not block the current submission.

### 3. Why HLA-specific rigor matters
HLA benchmarking requires evaluation conventions that are not standard in general genomics. The IMGT/HLA reference database is version-controlled and actively updated; allele designations in different database releases can diverge in ways that change whether a call is classified as correct or incorrect. PIHLA pins the IMGT/HLA database at version 3.59.0 throughout benchmarking and propagates this version through machine-readable metadata files, ensuring that truth normalisation, call normalisation, and accuracy evaluation all operate under the same nomenclature assumptions. At two-field resolution, allele-pair accuracy is evaluated as an exact pair match rather than as two independent allele calls, because partial credit for heterozygous loci would conflate genuinely ambiguous calls with outright errors. PIHLA also retains resolution-aware secondary metrics—three-field exact match rates and group-level agreement under G-group and P-group notations—for benchmarks where truth labels carry comparable suffix-encoded ambiguity information. These conventions are necessary for fair comparison across tools with different resolution profiles and for consistent reporting across benchmark updates as the IMGT/HLA database evolves.

### 4. Why workflow engineering matters
Reproducibility and portability are not supplementary concerns in this work; they are part of the methodological contribution. A benchmarking result that cannot be independently reproduced—because it depends on local database paths, environment-specific tool installations, or unversioned reference data—provides weaker scientific evidence than one that can be re-run from a frozen manifest and a containerised pipeline. PIHLA is implemented in Nextflow DSL2 with containerised tool modules that execute identically under Docker, Singularity, and HPC SLURM profiles. Benchmark runs are anchored to manifest-driven sample definitions, and all parameters—including IMGT/HLA version, abstention thresholds, and weight formula coefficients—are explicitly declared in versioned configuration files. This design means that the results reported here can be reproduced by other groups running the same pipeline version against the same 1000 Genomes inputs, and that the framework can be evaluated on new cohorts without modifying workflow code. Workflow engineering at this level also enables the benchmark to scale incrementally: new samples or new tools can be added to an existing run by updating manifests and resubmitting with `-resume`, without rebuilding completed outputs from scratch.

### 5. Relation to consHLA and prior work
The closest existing tool is consHLA (Jurtz et al.), which builds a consensus from multiple HLA callers using a rule-based voting strategy without confidence calibration or structured abstention. PIHLA extends that direction by adding benchmark-derived weight learning, calibration guardrails, abstention control, and a discordance taxonomy. Among single-tool benchmark papers, OptiType (Szolek et al., 2014) evaluated performance across 361 benchmark runs and set the precision standard for WES/WGS class I typing; arcasHLA (Orenbuch et al., 2019) demonstrated strong RNA-seq performance on 358 1000 Genomes individuals including DRB1 and DQB1 loci; T1K (Song et al., 2023) reported across 463 samples covering WGS, WES, and RNA-seq with broader locus coverage. The current PIHLA benchmarks are smaller in holdout scale than these tool papers and should not be positioned as a direct large-scale replacement for any single-tool evaluation. The target submission venue for the current manuscript is Bioinformatics, where the emphasis on infrastructure, reproducibility, and transparent scope control aligns with editorial expectations for methods and software papers. A higher-tier submission would require expanded holdout cohorts and broader locus coverage to be competitive.

### 6. Limitations
The most important limitation is the small size of the current holdout cohorts. The WGS wave1 holdout comprises 9 samples, the WES holdout 12 samples, and the RNA-seq holdout 7 samples. Wilson 95% CIs are correspondingly wide: WGS WeightedConsensus 0.34–0.69 and WES WeightedConsensus 0.78–0.97. These sizes are not competitive with the scale of individual tool benchmark papers such as OptiType (361 runs), arcasHLA (358 samples), or T1K (463 samples), and they do not support strong cross-modality superiority claims. The WGS wave provides the most mature quantitative evidence; WES and RNA should be treated as secondary results until larger matched holdout cohorts are available.

The WES underperformance of WeightedConsensus relative to MajorityVote (0.8611 versus 0.9444) is interpreted as consistent with a structural ceiling effect — in a high-accuracy WES regime where all single tools already agree, weighting cannot add value and can only reduce accuracy by shifting votes away from the consensus. Sensitivity analysis across three weight formulas confirms the WES result is stable at approximately 0.87 regardless of formula choice, arguing against a weight-calibration artefact. However, the ceiling-effect interpretation remains provisional because no direct uniform-weight ablation has been performed on the WES holdout; that ablation is a future-work item and does not block the current submission.

Two RNA tools are excluded from primary RNA comparisons: Seq2HLA (n=1 callable sample due to container failure) and SpecHLA RNA (6/100 samples completed due to incomplete pipeline run). These exclusions reflect pipeline coverage failures rather than tool quality differences and are handled under the phase-gated reporting policy.

The default weight formula (α=0.7 × base_reliability + β=0.3 × effective_confidence) is optimal for WES in the current benchmark but is sub-optimal for WGS in the trimodal sensitivity analysis, where all three alternative formulas produce higher accuracy (0.296–0.317 versus 0.267). This WGS result is a guardrail artefact rather than a principled confidence-weighting advantage: most WGS tools trigger the poor_calibration guardrail and fall back to base-reliability regardless of β, so varying β primarily changes the residual contribution of a few tools. The formula was chosen as a conservative default for WES/RNA stability and is not claimed to be globally optimal.

Current headline evaluation is restricted to HLA-A, -B, and -C. DRB1 and DQB1 are implemented in the framework but are not included in the primary benchmark because matched truth labels were unavailable in the current 1000 Genomes sample slice. Expanding to five-locus truth-backed evaluation — feasible given that complementary truth resources exist for 1000 Genomes individuals at these loci — is a high-value extension for future benchmark cycles. G-group and P-group ambiguity-aware evaluation is also currently blank for loci where matched group-suffixed truth labels are absent. Long-read and graph-aware backends remain future extensions of the workflow architecture.

### 7. Future directions
Several directions present clear opportunities to extend the current framework. Most immediately, native confidence extraction should be broadened beyond OptiType, T1K, ArcasHLA, HLA-HD, and Kourami to encompass all integrated callers; richer confidence coverage would allow the calibration guardrail to operate on a larger proportion of the ensemble vote and improve the discriminability of the effective-confidence layer. Expanding the benchmark design from the WGS-primary cohort into matched larger WES and RNA-seq cohorts would address the current asymmetry in holdout sizes: the WGS wave comprises 9 holdout samples while the WES and RNA holdouts contain 12 and 7 respectively, all with wide Wilson confidence intervals. A 50-sample matched tri-modal cohort is the nearest-term target for improving the statistical power of modality-level comparisons.

Beyond coverage, the framework would benefit from graph-aware and long-read backends that can resolve complex structural haplotypes missed by short-read alignment strategies. Adding population stratification layers—in which weight learning and abstention tuning are performed within ancestry groups rather than across a mixed cohort—would make PIHLA more useful for studies with heterogeneous ethnic composition and would allow calibration performance to be reported at population stratum resolution. On the interpretation side, richer discordance taxonomy for tumour-derived samples, where somatic loss of heterozygosity, copy-number changes, or tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte contamination can explain cross-modality disagreements, would extend the clinical utility of the tool. Finally, broader integration with downstream immunoinformatics interfaces—including neoantigen prediction pipelines, HLA ligandome databases, and transplant compatibility scoring tools—would reduce the integration burden for translational users who currently must bridge the gap between HLA typing output and downstream analysis manually.

## Conclusion
PIHLA demonstrates that HLA typing from next-generation sequencing data benefits from a reliability-weighted ensemble with empirical confidence guardrails, rather than simple majority voting. On a 42-sample 1000 Genomes WGS benchmark with frozen holdout splits, guarded weighted consensus matched the accuracy of the best single tool (OptiType, 0.5185 overall correct-call rate on the nine-sample holdout) while substantially improving callable rate relative to equal-weight majority vote (0.9259 versus 0.7778). In the full tri-modal cohort, WES and RNA-seq holdout results reached 0.9167 and 1.000 respectively, though the WES underperformance relative to majority vote (0.8611 versus 0.9444) indicates that weighted consensus is most beneficial in regimes where tool agreement is imperfect. The key mechanism is the calibration guardrail: in the WGS benchmark, three of five tools with confidence data showed poor empirical calibration and were blocked from confidence-based weight boosting, demonstrating that raw tool confidence cannot be used as a direct proxy for reliability without benchmark-time validation.

The broader contribution of this work is methodological. By combining benchmark-derived weight learning, empirical calibration guardrails, resolution-aware HLA evaluation, and structured discordance tagging within a reproducible Nextflow workflow, PIHLA provides a platform that can be deployed, benchmarked, and extended without the operational fragmentation that characterises current HLA typing practice. The transparency of the framework—explicit phase-gated reporting, machine-readable metadata, frozen benchmark splits, and pinned IMGT/HLA versioning—means that the reported results are independently reproducible and that limitations are surfaced rather than suppressed.

Looking ahead, the priority is to expand holdout cohort sizes for WES and RNA-seq modalities, extend native confidence extraction to remaining integrated tools, and validate the framework against cohorts with broader ancestry representation and richer truth labels at three-field resolution. Together these extensions will sharpen the statistical basis for modality-level claims and strengthen the case for PIHLA as a general-purpose reliability-weighted HLA ensemble platform with confidence guardrails, suitable for both methodological benchmarking and translational immunogenomics workflows.
